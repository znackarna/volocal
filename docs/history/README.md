the later entry wins and the earlier one still explains what was believed at

**[2026-08-17](2026-08-17.md)** — While a transcript runs the mark turns ninety degrees and stops being a face: the two `o` become rollers, the stem becomes sheet metal passing between them, and the smile leaves because a machine does not smile until it is done. Nothing is drawn for it — the same four shapes from another side, which is the only reason the idea holds, since anything that had to be added would be a second mark rather than a state of the first. Three things the movement needed: the sheet travels along y in unturned coordinates, because it is the parent's rotation that makes the journey horizontal and along x it would run lengthwise through the rollers instead of between them; it turns about the centre of the drawing rather than of the frame, and comes down by 26.2 / 30.41 because upright the drawing is longer than its frame is tall; and it sets off with the turn rather than after it, the rule being not *do not set off early* but *do not reach the rollers early*. When a transcript finishes the loop is not cut — a pass runs from +26 to −26, so the nose is exactly halfway, and stopping means waiting for that halfway point and dropping the animation there, so the last sheet through stays as the nose. And the smile returns by turning about the centre of its own arc, which makes it slide along the curve it is made of rather than travel in a straight line; it cannot leave by turning alone, being always the same distance from that centre, so the turn carries the movement and a fade carries the departure. It lives in the drop zone, which stands at the top of the archive whether or not the list below it is empty. What is deliberately not answered: the loop says that something is happening, not how far along it is. Then the header's two states were given to the screens rather than to a clock: the archive rests on the whole name and shrinks under the pointer, a transcript rests on the mark and opens under it. The ten-second introduction went with that and had to — on a screen that rests open it would have contradicted itself inside those ten seconds — and nothing of the idea is lost, because the crop still happens on the way into a transcript, where a reader causes it instead of a timer. And a rule about numbering: a release moves by one patch, and a minor is asked for rather than inferred — grading a release with its version number mistakes an order for a grade, when what it contained is in the change log and in this folder. Last, the header inversion was taken back on the owner's word — it did not work under a hand. The argument for it was sound on paper, but a resting state that shrinks when you approach it is a control that retreats, and the archive's mark is a button people aim at, so the one gesture that should have shown more took something away. The mill stays; the record of the reverted change stays too, because a log that quietly removes the decisions it later dropped cannot be trusted about the ones it kept.
