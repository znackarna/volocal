### 2026-08-26 — The FAQ animation was Chromium-only, and quietly so

He reported that the published page jumps straight to the open answer, and sent
the page saved off the site so it could be read rather than guessed at. The CSS
in it is intact — so the fault was never a deploy, a bundle or a lost rule.

**The rule is Chromium's.** `interpolate-size: allow-keywords` and
`::details-content`, both from 2024 and both still only in Chrome and Edge, were
what made the answer slide. `@supports` sat around them, as
[2026-08-25](2026-08-25.md) said, so that a browser without them would keep the
instant open — which sounded careful and was the trap: the page animated for
whoever built it and jumped for Firefox and Safari, and said nothing about it
either way. A fallback nobody can see is indistinguishable from a fault.

The earlier entry's argument still holds — the height a `<details>` opens to is
not a number anybody wrote down. **A script can measure it.** The panel's own
height is animated from what it is to what it becomes: `open` goes on before the
measurement and comes off after the collapse, the overflow is hidden only while
something moves, and `.is-closing` turns the chevron at the start of the way
shut instead of a quarter of a second after it.

Three things in it are there for a reason and each is written beside the code:
`fill: both`, or the last frame of a collapse paints the answer at full height
before `open` comes off; a timer of the same length as a backstop, because the
whole state of the panel hangs off one callback and a sleeping tab that drops it
would leave the answer open, clipped and impossible to shut; and a token, so a
click that has been overtaken cannot settle a panel that now belongs to a later
one.

Without JavaScript the disclosure is a `<details>` and opens at once — the same
place the `@supports` was falling back to, now the fallback for having no script
rather than for using Firefox. Reduced motion still gets the browser's own
toggle.

Measured rather than watched: closed 55 px, half way through opening 117,
open 136; then 136, half way shut 72, and back to 55 with `open` off, no classes
left on the element and no inline height.

### 2026-08-26 — The veil blurs the window instead of reading it back

He hovered a carousel arrow and straight lines appeared across the blurred
window behind the dialog. Firefox and the artifact preview both, which was the
useful half of the report: two engines, one cause.

`backdrop-filter` has to read the pixels behind it, blur them and paint them
back. The only place on this page where that happens is inside `.preview-stage`
— a subtree scaled to the column and deliberately promoted to a layer of its
own, for reasons written down on 2026-08-19 and still good. There the read comes
off a texture rasterised in tiles, each tile blurred from what is inside it and
nothing beyond, so the seams between tiles stand as straight lines the moment
part of that region is repainted. The pointer on an arrow is enough: its
background transitions, the region repaints, the lines appear. Both engines
rasterise in tiles, so both show it.

**A `filter` on the window reads nothing back.** It is rasterised once, with the
whole window to blur from, and a repaint anywhere else cannot reach it. The
edges soften into `.shot`, which is painted in the same ground colour, so the
frame survives.

The first attempt at it named `.app` as the thing to blur and left two of the
four slides sharp: in two of them the veil is a sibling of `.app`, in the other
two it sits inside `.app` beside `<main>`. The rule now asks the veil where it
lives — every child of its parent except the veil itself — and all four are
checked rather than assumed: four veils, one sibling each, each blurred, and no
`backdrop-filter` left on any of them.

The archive's dissolve strips came off the backdrop as well. They are the
application's own rules, so they are switched off inside the previews rather
than edited in the generated stylesheet: `::before` keeps its colour fade, which
its comment says carries the effect alone, and `::after` was nothing but blur.
A dissolve one shade weaker is a smaller wrong than a seam under a scrolling
list.

He chose this of three; the other two were dropping the blur in the previews
altogether, and keeping it while asking the dialog for a layer of its own —
which sometimes works, and *sometimes* is not a reason that can be written down.

### 2026-08-26 — The page in English, made rather than kept

The presentation page now exists at two addresses: `/` in Czech and `/en/` in
English. What is not new is a second page — **`index.html` is still the only one
in the repository**, and the English one is written from it at deploy time by
`docs/site/translate.mjs` out of `docs/site/en.json`.

**Why not two files.** Both halves of a bilingual document are kept by hand
here, and `docs-check.mjs` counts them, which works because a README is
paragraphs. This page is 4 600 lines of layout that a sentence sits inside; two
copies of it would not disagree about a word, they would disagree about the
page. So the layout is authored once and only the sentences are doubled.

**Keys are the Czech sentences.** No `data-t` on anything, so the markup stays a
page rather than a template. The price is that rewriting a Czech sentence
orphans its translation, and that is the half worth having: `--check` runs
inside `npm run docs:check`, so an untranslated sentence stops a build instead
of reaching a reader in the wrong language. It checks both directions — an entry
nobody uses any more is reported too, rather than sitting there looking
translated.

Three things needed naming rather than looking up.

**The invented transcript is a `<span>` per word**, because the words light up
one at a time as the recording plays. Keyed by text, *si* would have to be the
same English word in three sentences that do not share one. Those 243 spans
carry their English on themselves in `data-en`, which the generator uses and
then removes. The English reads as English and the timings are untouched: what
changes is which word sits in which span, and a span is not a translation unit.

**The version moves every release.** Keyed as it stands, *Verze 1.2.21 pro
Windows 10 a 11* would orphan its own translation on the day 1.2.22 went out, so
a `{version}` takes its place while the sentence is looked up. The deploy then
rewrites the number in both pages from the latest release — two mentions in each,
asserted, so a reworded sentence fails the build rather than shipping last
month's number.

**A `<script>` is carried through untouched**, which is right for 400 lines of
animation and wrong for the four strings those scripts say out loud. They read
`document.documentElement.lang` and answer in the page's own language.

**The English is the application's English.** The labels in the preview windows
are taken from `src/locales/en` — Archive, Speakers, Review, Quick tips,
Faithful edit, *More accurate, or faster?* — so the window on the English page is
the window an English reader will actually see. The prose is written as English,
not as translated Czech, which is what `CLAUDE.md` already asks of every document
with two halves.

**Nobody is redirected.** A visible switch in the header, `hreflang` on both
pages, and `x-default` pointing at Czech. His call, and the reason is the one
that always wins for a link: a shared address should open what it says it opens.

The English page is served a directory deeper and looks one level up for the
stylesheet, the scripts and the fonts, so `en/` holds one file and the 600 kB of
fonts are not on the site twice.

Verified by rehearsing the deploy locally: both pages written, the version
rewritten in both, and the English one rendered — hero, header, the app window
in English, the fonts and the brand animation all arriving from `../`.

### 2026-08-26 — The English lede stops saying *It*

He read the first paragraph in both languages and said the Czech was the better
copy. It was, and the reason is one word repeated.

Czech drops the subject, so the lede is three bare verbs in a row —
*Rozpozná. Opraví. Zpracuje.* — and they land like a drumbeat. The English put
the subject back in each of them: *It tells speakers apart. It fixes mistakes.
It handles any audio format…* Three sentences, three `It`s, and what reads as
rhythm in Czech reads as a stutter in English.

English can drop the subject too, in exactly this register:

> Volocal transcribes speech with language models that run on your own computer.
> Tells speakers apart. Fixes mistakes. Handles any audio format, video or an
> online stream. Free. Without losing control or privacy.

*Without giving up control or privacy* went with it: the Czech is *bez ztráty*,
a loss rather than a surrender, and nobody is giving anything up in it.

The description meta carries the same three verbs and got the same treatment,
and one more calque went with them: *a safe and blazing-fast stack is the
ground floor* was a building nobody was standing in. It is *the foundation*,
which is what *je základ* says.

This is the rule the documents already carry — each half written as if a person
wrote it in that language — arriving at the page. Worth the entry because the
fault was invisible from the Czech side: every sentence was translated, and the
paragraph was still worse.


**A pass over the whole page followed**, at his ask that the English be as good
a copy as the Czech rather than a faithful translation of it. Sixteen lines
changed and none of them were wrong before; they were merely translated.

The faults were of four kinds, and each is worth naming because each will
happen again:

**A calque nobody says.** *Structured conversations, yes; chaotic panel
discussions, rather not* — *rather not* is *spíš ne* wearing English clothes.
It reads as *not really* here.

**Formality the Czech does not have.** *What shall we transcribe today?* for
*Co spolu dnes přepíšeme?* — the Czech is warm and in the plural of two people
doing something together; *What are we transcribing today?* is that, and
*shall* was a translator being polite.

**A word repeated where the Czech varied it.** *See what it was not sure of*
sat above *Volocal marks the spots it was unsure of*; the Czech says *nejistá*
and then *sporná*. It is *See where it hesitated* now. The limits section had
the same echo — *limits* in the heading and again in the sentence under it,
where the Czech goes *limity* then *omezení*.

**Length where the Czech is short.** *Once it is set up, you can work without
the internet* is a sentence about the internet; *you can work offline* is the
same fact in three words. *From three quarters to double* became *from
three-quarter speed to double*, because the Czech noun carries a speed and the
English number does not.

Two puns survived the crossing rather than being explained: *Poznámky drží
slovo* is *Notes that hold their word* — a note is pinned to a word, and the
phrase means keeping a promise in both languages — and *Složky na úložky*, which
rhymes in Czech and cannot, is *A place for everything*, which is a saying an
English reader finishes for themselves.

### 2026-08-26 — The English stops saying *your* three times as often as the Czech

He asked what *your* was doing in the headline. Counted rather than argued
about: the Czech page carries six possessives, the English carried fourteen.
English marketing copy reaches for *your* the way Czech does not, and a
translation that does it is not wrong sentence by sentence — it is wrong by the
page.

Six of them went, and the headline is the one worth reading twice:

> Zapněte počítač. Vypněte předplatné. → **Computer on. Subscription off.**

The Czech is an imperative with a bare noun, which English cannot do — *turn on
computer* is not a sentence — so the translation reached for *your* to make the
grammar work and paid two words for it. Dropping the verb drops the need: two
nouns, two particles, and the contrast the line exists for is the whole of it.
What it gives up is the address to the reader; the lede under it is in the
second person and picks that back up straight away.

The others were plainer. *Data neopustí počítač* is *the* computer, not *your*
computer, twice. *Napříč firmou* is *across the company* — and that one was
his own decision this morning, when *vaše* came off the people, arriving in the
English an hour later anyway. And the lede said *language models that run on
your own computer* where the Czech says *lokálních*: *local language models* is
the same word count as the Czech and the same claim.

Eight remain, and each has a Czech possessive standing opposite it or is the

company* spends four words on what *napříč firmou* says in two.

the Czech, so the English now wraps where the Czech wraps.

The wider lesson is the one to keep: **a sentence that has to share a row with a
button is a measurement, not only a translation.** Nothing in `docs:check` can
catch that — it counts translations, not their width.
